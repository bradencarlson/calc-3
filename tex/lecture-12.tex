\subsection{Limits and Continuity}

\content{
Much as in the case of function of a single variable, we can take limits of
functions of two variables. Here is the formal definition:
\begin{definition} If $f(x,y)$ is a function of two variables, and $(a,b)$ is a
point in the interior of the domain $D$ of $f$, then we say that the limit of
$f(x,y)$ as $(x,y)$ approaches $(a,b)$ is $L$ if for every number $\epsilon >
0$, there exists a $\delta>0$ such that whenever 
$$ \sqrt{(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2}< \delta, $$
we have that $|f(x,y)-L|<\epsilon$, and we write 
$$ \lim_{(x,y) \rightarrow (a,b)} f(x,y) = L. $$
\end{definition}

\content{
\begin{example} Show that the following limit does not exist:
$$ \lim_{(x,y)\rightarrow (0,0)} \frac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}. $$
\end{example}

\content{
\begin{theorem} The properties of the limit which hold for functions of a single
variable also hold for functions of two variables: 
\begin{enumerate}
\item The limit of a sum is the sum of the limits. 
\item THe limit of a difference is the difference of the limits. 
\item The limit of a constant times a function is the constant times the limit
of the function. 
\item The limit of a product is the product of the limits. 
\item The limit of a quotient is the quotient of the limits, provided that the
limit of the denominator is not zero. 
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\content{
\begin{definition} A function $f$ of two variables is called continuous at
$(a,b)$ if 
$$ \lim_{(x,y)\rightarrow (a,b)} f(x,y) = f(a,b). $$
We say that $f$ is continous on $D$ is $f$ is continuous at each point of $D$. 
\end{definition}
}{% presentation
}{% nopresentation
}{% comments
Note that polynomials are continuous, so are rational functions (on their
domain). 
}
